I wrote a small tool to intervene on individual neurons in a 0.8-billion-parameter language model and inspect how its outputs changed. Some results were fairly predictable; others were less so. I also misread one of the experiments on my first pass. This post walks through what I tested and what I found.

Model: Qwen3.5-0.8B / 4B / 9B · Tool: my repo, nudge · all quotes below are the model's actual output

I'd wanted to try this for a while. Language models contain millions of internal units, and asking what a particular neuron does often has no straightforward answer. I built nudge (which is inspired by this repo) to intervene on one neuron at a time by adding a small offset to its activation, then compare the model's response with and without that intervention. I didn't use a scoring metric or an embedding-based comparison here; I inspected the outputs directly. If a response changed in a consistent, interpretable way, that gave me a reason to investigate the neuron further.

How I actually did it

The procedure is simple. For each neuron, I run the same prompt twice and compare the outputs:

# 1. Ask the model a question normally (the "baseline")
# 2. Ask again, but with a small extra push to ONE neuron (the "steered" run)

nudge check L8.N2047 --run runs/manual-qwen35 --dose 0.5 \
        --query "The book I lost in 2019 had a blue cover and exactly 320 pages. What was its title?"

# → prints the baseline answer, the steered answer, and how different they are

The main parameter is the dose — a multiplier, not an additive offset. For each neuron I build a steering vector of magnitude dose × R_layer pointing along that neuron's own down-projection direction ŵᵢ = W_down[:,i] / ‖W_down[:,i]‖₂, where R_layer is the median per-token ℓ₂-norm of the layer's MLP output, estimated once over a calibration corpus. That vector is then added to the residual stream at every token position. So dose = 0.5 injects a perturbation of half the layer's median activation magnitude in the neuron's own direction. Because the magnitude is anchored to R_layer and the direction to the neuron itself, the same dose is a comparable intervention across neurons. Same model, prompt, and decoding throughout — the only thing that changes is this single injected component on the residual stream.

First, the baseline model gets some basic facts wrong

Before looking at interventions, I checked the model's baseline responses. The 0.8B model made several basic factual errors, often without expressing uncertainty. A few examples:

Qwen3.5-0.8Bwrong, confident
"…when the Soviet Union's Vostok 1 mission landed the first human on the Moon. The mission was led by Yuri Gagarin…"
Qwen3.5-0.8Bwrong, confident
"The first President of the United States was George Washington. He served as the 7th President from 1789…"

Vostok 1 was the first human spaceflight, not a Moon landing; that was Apollo 11 in 1969. Washington was the first US president, not the seventh. The model also answered Augustus to the question about the Roman emperor in the cat riddle, and identified a supposedly lost book as "The Book of Mormon" by Joseph Smith. These baseline errors matter: they provide examples where an intervention might change the model's answer, for better or worse.

A neuron that shifts the model towards refusal

One of the clearest effects appeared on a question the model could not answer from the information provided: "The book I lost in 2019 had a blue cover and exactly 320 pages. What was its title?" In the baseline run, it invents a title. I found that intervening on L8.N2047 changes the response. The control below shows the output at several dose values:

dose 0.5
Qwen3.5-0.8B

Below 0.5, the neuron mostly changes which incorrect title the model guesses (Book of Mormon → Book of the Dead). At around 0.5, the response changes to a refusal: "I don't have access to the specific book you lost, as I don't have access to your personal library…". The transition is sharp in the dose range I tested: at 0.45 it guesses, while at 0.5 and 0.55 it refuses. That is a notably clear change for an intervention on a single unit.

The effect did not appear to disrupt facts the model already knew. When I asked "What's the capital of France?" with the neuron enabled, it still answered Paris. In these tests, the intervention shifted responses to questions about inaccessible personal information without obviously degrading this unrelated factual answer.

A second neuron with a similar effect

I found a second neuron with a similar effect, L11.N1792. Intervening on both neurons makes the refusal more elaborate. The examples below compare the single-neuron and two-neuron interventions:

The two interventions appear to reinforce a similar response, but this does not mean that either neuron represents honesty on its own. The behaviour is also present in the baseline: on my set of 10 personal-information questions, the model refused 8 without any steering. The intervention seems to increase the likelihood or strength of that response in the tested examples.

A neuron column that changes factual details

Another pattern appeared across a column of neurons around N3071 in layers 11–19. Intervening on these units changes which specific details the model cites, including dates, counts, and names. The effect is mixed. I tested it on 10 facts with known answers: the intervention corrected some responses, corrupted others, and left the rest unchanged. Select a card to compare examples:

baseline (before nudge)
N3071 @ 0.8 (after)

Across the 10 tests, the result was 5 corrected · 2 corrupted · 3 neutral. The intervention moved the Moon-landing answer towards the correct mission, but it also changed Lindbergh into a British naval officer and introduced an incorrect claim about Marseille being a capital. This is not a general-purpose accuracy improvement. It changes which details the model produces, and whether that helps depends on the prompt. I would describe it as a fact-perturbation risk, not a corrective mechanism.

What happens when you intervene on the final layer

In this model, the final layer (layer 23 of 24) is close to the output logits. At a strong dose, intervening on the neurons I tested there caused the model to enter repetition loops. The practical lesson from these tests is to avoid strong interventions in the final layer. Perturbing activations close to the output can destabilise generation. A milder version of this effect also appeared in the 9B model, although less consistently.

Comparing models at different scales

After identifying a few effects in the 0.8B model, I ran the same checks on Qwen3.5-4B and 9B. Two things stood out. The larger models were more accurate on the factual questions: for example, the 9B model identifies Apollo 11 correctly without intervention, leaving less room for the fact-shifting effect to improve the answer. I also had to revise one of my initial conclusions:

My initial conclusion was wrong. In the first pass, the larger models appeared more willing to answer personal questions, suggesting that the privacy boundary weakened with scale. The issue was the generation length: the larger models began with a chain-of-thought preamble (for example, "Okay, let me think about this… I don't have access to personal information…"), and my short output window truncated the response before the refusal. They were not answering the question; they were reasoning through it and then refusing. After rerunning the tests with a longer output window, the apparent reversal disappeared.

With that correction, the result is straightforward: all three models refused the personal-information questions in this test set. The difference was mainly in how they expressed the refusal. Takeaways

Few things stood out from these experiments:

  • The smaller model makes confident factual errors. The 0.8B model said Vostok 1 landed on the Moon and that Washington was the seventh president. Interventions changed these answers, but didn't improve them, which was kinda obvious.
  • A single neuron can strengthen a refusal response. In the tested prompt, L8.N2047 at a dose of around 0.5 changed a fabricated personal-information answer into a refusal, without changing the answer to an unrelated factual question. L11.N1792 produced a similar effect.
  • A changed fact is not necessarily a corrected fact. The N3071 column changes which details the model cites. Some changes are correct; others introduce errors. Treat it as a perturbation risk, not an accuracy improvement.
  • Avoid strong interventions in the final layer. In these tests, they caused repetition loops. Specific neuron coordinates also do not transfer across model sizes, even when some broader behaviours appear in more than one model.

Want to try it yourself?

The experiments use my nudge repository. To run a check, the command looks like this:

nudge check L8.N2047 --run runs/manual-qwen35 --dose 0.5 --query "your question here"

Run the prompt once with the intervention and once without it, then compare the outputs. Each check is easy to inspect: rather than relying on a single score, you can read the generated text and judge what changed. That is what I did across the batches described above. I am also working on improving it by integrating agentic checks where you only define a semantic concept like "a neuron responsible for making the lm sound like a zombie", and the agent will go throguh each individual neuron (or pairs of neuron) to see which are responsible.
