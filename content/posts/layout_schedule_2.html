Ratio study · layout_schedule_2 · 330 runs · 4×H100

I compared seven attention-to-MLP ratios in 48-sublayer decoders. At first, the MLP-heavy models looked better on language modelling, but they also had more parameters. Once I matched model size, most of that gap disappeared. Retrieval gave a different answer: the balanced split was the most reliable.

7 ratio points × 3 seeds × 4 layouts · d=512 · 48 sublayers · edu_mix (FineWeb, 531M tok) · 6k–16k steps

TL;DR
  • For language modelling, the raw sweep favors MLP-heavy layouts. Validation loss is similar across the MLP-heavy end, then worsens as the models become more attention-heavy. The best-to-worst gap is 0.0744, about 8.8 times the measured noise floor.
  • Most of that gap tracks model size. MLP sublayers have about twice the parameters of attention sublayers. In a separate control near 80M parameters, the spread across ratios shrinks to 0.0132. A stepwise comparison of the raw best and worst runs attributes about 95% of that particular gap to size changes.
  • Retrieval favors a different balance. In the synthetic recall task, 1:1 is the only ratio where every run clears the pass threshold. The other ratios have mixed outcomes across runs.

My takeaway: model size explains most of the language-modelling trend in this sweep; for this recall task, the balance between attention and MLPs also matters.

01

The question: does more attention help?

Every decoder block in a conventional transformer does two things in turn: an attention sublayer (global, token-to-token mixing) and an MLP sublayer (token-local nonlinear transformation). With a 4× MLP expansion, roughly two-thirds of the sublayers are MLP and one-third are attention. I wanted to know what happens if you change that proportion on purpose.

I started with a straightforward prediction: perhaps standard transformers devote too much computation to token-local processing. Because attention lets each token use information from other positions, adding more attention might improve retrieval and language modelling—until the model has too little MLP capacity left to transform features. That prediction implies an inverted-U, with its peak on the attention-heavy side.

I also considered two alternatives. MLP capacity might matter more than expected, or different tasks might favor different mixes. I designed the experiments to distinguish those possibilities.

I compare the language-modelling results with retrieval tasks, then use a parameter-matched control to separate the layer mix from model size.

02

What the ratio means—and why it is hard to test

First, what do I mean by a ratio? Each of the 48 positions in my layout string contains one sublayer: attention or MLP. Throughout this post, ratios are written MLP:attention, so 2:1 means two MLP sublayers for every attention sublayer. The figure below show all the representative ratios. Each uses the evenly spread alt arrangement.

There is an important complication. An attention sublayer and an MLP sublayer are not the same size. With d=512 and a SwiGLU expansion of ~1376:

attention sublayer  = 4·d²           ≈ 1.05M params   (Q,K,V,O)   26.2M FLOPs/tok
MLP sublayer       = 3·d·d_ff   ≈ 2.11M params   (w_gate, w_up, w_down)   52.4M FLOPs/tok

So every time I move one sublayer from MLP to attention, the model gets ~1M parameters and ~26M FLOPs/token lighter. Sweeping the ratio from 4:1 to 1:4 (holding the 48-sublayer total fixed) therefore changes the model from 95.0M → 65.2M parameters and from 585.7M → 450.8M FLOPs/token. The attention-heavy end is smaller and cheaper.

Why the parameter count matters

There is a useful tension in this comparison. If attention-heavy turns out to be better, it would be a free lunch — better and cheaper. If it is worse, it is a double loss — worse and it was the cheap one. Either way the comparison is clean. But it also means a "ratio effect" I measure on this grid is automatically entangled with a size effect, because the two move together. Untangling that is the whole job of §6, and it is the crux of the study.

There is a second, subtler confound: where the attention sublayers sit. "16 attention + 32 MLP" can be evenly spread, clustered at the front, or clustered at the back, and those are different machines (front-loaded attention changes early feature extraction; back-loaded attention changes late integration). So I did not test one arrangement per ratio — I tested several (an evenly-spread alt plus fixed-seed random permutations) so that a ratio effect that survives arrangement changes is a ratio effect, not a position effect. §7 quantifies how large the position effect is.

Reporting convention

I report final validation loss (the value at the last eval, which is also the best-achieved for these runs) unless noted, because that is the number a practitioner would actually ship. "params" includes embeddings; the within-family param differences are all in the transformer, so they are the real size effect. Lower loss is better.

Three things stand out, and they all point the same way:

(a) The shape is wrong for the hypothesis. There is no peak on the attention-heavy side. Instead: a flat, near-tie plateau on the MLP-heavy side (4:1, 3:1, 2:1 all within ~1σ of each other; the conventional 2:1 is the single best point), then a monotone, accelerating degradation as you add attention past 2:1. The step differences going attention-heavy (2:1→1:1→1:2→1:3→1:4) are +0.011, +0.011, +0.020, +0.033 — all positive, and growing. That is a clean monotone trend, not noise, and it is the opposite of the inverted-U I was hunting for.

(b) The signal is large relative to the noise. Within-ratio σ (the noise floor) is ~0.008; best–worst is 0.0744. The two attention-heavy extremes (36A, 38A) sit >4σ above the plateau. This is not a one-seed accident — it reproduces across all 12 runs per ratio.

(c) The worst config is also the least stable. 1:4 (38A/10M) has the highest variance (σ=0.0213, ~2× the plateau) — the attention-heavy models not only lose on average, they are less reproducible. And the gradient-norm traces confirm it: the attention-heavy end shows higher p95 gradient norms (0.75 at 38A vs 0.54–0.56 on the MLP-heavy side) and the one training spike in the whole core sweep lands on an attention-heavy config (36A). Attention-heavy is both slower and less stable.

Takeaway (core sweep)

On language modelling, more attention is not better. The curve is flat-and-fine on the MLP-heavy side and monotonically worse as attention is added past 1:1, with the worst (most attention-heavy) config also the least stable. The "attention-heavy is the optimum" branch of the hypothesis is, at first glance, dead.

But hold on

Remember §2: on this grid, MLP-heavy = more parameters. "MLP-heavy is better" could be a ratio effect or simply "the bigger model wins." The attention-heavy configs are the smaller ones (65–72M) and the MLP-heavy ones the larger (89–95M). I cannot call this a ratio effect until I hold size constant. That is the next section, and it changes the conclusion substantially.

05

Result 2 — retrieval: 1:1 is the only reliable split

The hypothesis was, in part, a retrieval story — attention does global token-to-token mixing, so "more attention should help retrieval." I tested that directly with three synthetic probes, each a different flavour of "find the right token": 1-hop (follow a pointer), local (a rule within a small window), and recall (recall a specific item from context). The two easy ones saturate and carry no signal; the recall task is where the ratio actually shows through.

Read this carefully, because it is the cleanest task-dependence evidence in the study and it refutes the simple "attention = retrieval" story. If more attention helped retrieval, the attention-heavy ratios (1:2, 1:3, 1:4) should be the best and most reliable. They are not — they are bimodal, just like the MLP-heavy side. The pattern is not "more attention is better." It is "1:1 is the sweet spot": recall needs a specific balance of attention (global search) and MLP (feature transformation), and 1:1 is the only split that reliably has both. The MLP-heavy extreme (4:1) has the most failures, and the attention-heavy 1:2 is also bimodal — so it is "just enough of both," not "maximise attention."

And note the cross-task tension, which is the heart of the "task-dependent" branch: 2:1 is the best ratio for language modelling (Fig. 2) but bimodal/unreliable for recall (mean 0.612, three runs below the pass bar), while 1:1 — only slightly worse for LM (2.702 vs 2.690) — is the only reliable ratio for recall. Different tasks, different optimal compositions. There is no single universal ratio.

Takeaway (retrieval)

The ratio genuinely matters for retrieval, and the optimum is 1:1 (balanced), defined by reliability (every seed passes), not just mean accuracy. The results suggest a composition effect in this retrieval family: the most reliable ratio is not the largest model. I do not treat this small-scale comparison as proof that size can never interact with the split.

06

Separating ratio from size: most of the raw gap is size

This is the key control, so I will spell out the comparison. The core sweep (§4) holds the 48-sublayer total fixed and varies the ratio. But because an MLP sublayer carries ~2× the parameters of an attention sublayer (§2), varying the ratio at fixed sublayer-count also varies the parameter count: the MLP-heavy end is 89–95M, the attention-heavy end 65–72M. So the 0.0744 "ratio effect" I measured could be:

  • a genuine ratio effect (the composition of attention vs MLP matters), or
  • a size effect (MLP-heavy just has more parameters, and bigger is better), or
  • some mixture of both.

To separate them, I ran the parameter-matched control: for each ratio I retuned the MLP expansion (d_ff) so that every ratio lands at the same ~80M total, then varied only the composition. If the ratio effect survives at fixed size, it is real. If it vanishes, the core-sweep effect was size all along.

Now contrast this with the recall task (§5), where the best ratio (1:1) is not the largest model — in fact the largest (4:1) is the worst (0.588). So for recall, the ratio effect appears to depend on the composition itself, unlike the language-modeling result where size explains most of the gap. What the size-matched comparison tells us

The answer to "is there a specific optimal attention:MLP ratio?" is task-dependent, and for the primary task (LM) it is largely a matter of size, not ratio. For language modelling you can spend your parameter budget on the split however you like (within the tested range) — what matters is having more parameters. The apparent "MLP-heavy optimum" of §4 is an artifact of spending that budget on more (larger) sublayers. For retrieval, the composition is what matters: a balanced 1:1 split is what reliably delivers the capability.

Robustness: depth, finer grid, longer training, stability

A finding I don't trust is one that only holds at one depth, one grid resolution, and one step budget. I checked all three, plus training stability. These checks broadly support the same pattern.

The MLP-heavy-is-best, attention-heavy-is-worst ordering is reproduced at a different depth and width. 7.2 Finer grid — where is the plateau edge?

I filled the coarse gaps (14, 18, 28, 34 attention) to locate the edge of the MLP-heavy plateau. Results: 14A=2.6975, 16A=2.6902, 18A=2.6892 (best), 28A=2.7000, 34A=2.7255. So the best point is 18A, essentially tied with the 16A core point (Δ=0.001); 14A is slightly worse but within ~1σ; and the curve degrades clearly on the attention-heavy side (28A is +0.011, 34A is +0.036 over the plateau). So the "optimal" region for LM is not a sharp point at exactly 2:1 — it is a broad plateau around 16–18A (MLP:Attn ≈ 2:1 down to ~1.7:1), and I should read "optimal" as that plateau, not a single ratio.

The spatial arrangement of the sublayers is a secondary effect. For most ratios the spread across the four layouts is 0.006–0.016 (small, within a couple of σ). It is largest at the attention-heavy extreme (38A, spread 0.0442, where some placements are notably bad — r1 and r2 are ~0.04 worse than alt). If position were the real driver, the four layouts per ratio would have scattered randomly; they don't.

Robustness verdict

The core story — MLP-heavy is best / attention-heavy is worst on LM, and that's mostly a size effect; retrieval favors a balanced split — holds across depth (48→24 sublayers), across a finer grid, across 2× the step budget, and across four spatial arrangements. It is not an artifact of any single choice I made.

Does a specific optimal ratio exist? For language modelling, no — the apparent "optimal ratio" is a size effect. For the tested retrieval task, 1:1 is the most reliable split.

  • Language modelling (the primary objective): the apparent optimum sits at ~2:1 (MLP-heavy), but it is confounded with size (MLP-heavy = more parameters). The parameter-matched control shows that at fixed size the ratio has almost no effect (spread 0.013, within the noise floor). So for LM, what matters is total parameter count, not the attention:MLP ratio. The "MLP-heavy optimum" is an artifact of spending the parameter budget on more (larger) sublayers.
  • Retrieval (recall): the 1:1 split is the most reliable in this run set. This suggests a composition effect, though the comparison does not rule out size interactions across model scales.

Across both tasks the relationship is also asymmetric, and I think that is the most durable single finding here: you can remove attention (move toward more MLP) with little cost — 4:1, 3:1, 2:1 are all within noise of each other on LM — but you cannot add attention (move toward less MLP) without paying, in both LM loss and, sharply, in retrieval reliability. MLP-heavy is the safe side; attention-heavy is the costly side.

Interpretation. The load-bearing work that looks like "MLP capacity" is actually total capacity (parameter count) for language modelling. At a fixed size, how you split it between attention and MLP matters far less for LM than the hypothesis assumed — the model can absorb a wide range of compositions. But for retrieval the split itself matters: a balanced 1:1 reliably delivers the global-search capability, while both the MLP-heavy and the attention-heavy extremes fail on some seeds. Conventional wisdom is right that you need sufficient capacity; it is wrong to think the attention:MLP ratio is the key dial for LM. For LM it is the size dial.

What could confound this result—and how I checked

Because the effect sizes are small, I want to be explicit about what could have fooled me, and what I did about each. I spell out the main limitations below.

There are still limits to what these runs can tell me:

  • Single width family per task. One width (d=512) for the LM core; one width (d=128) for the recall probe. I vary depth and size within each, but not both simultaneously. A full width × depth × ratio grid is the natural next step.
  • The "genuine" ratio effect on LM is small and wiggly. At fixed size the pm curve is a shallow wiggle (16A and 32A both ~2.70, extremes slightly worse) — real but weak, and not a clean monotone story. I treat this as a small effect within the noise, not as evidence for a precise secondary optimum.
  • No generation / qualitative metrics. I report loss + task EM. If attention-heavy models differ in how they fail (e.g. worse long-range coherence at equal loss), that is not characterised here.
  • Retrieval probes are synthetic and coarse. The stronger real-world probe is needle-in-haystack at long context; my recall task is a controlled stand-in. The direction of the finding (balanced > either extreme) is what I'd expect to transfer, but the exact 1:1 optimum could shift with a harder retrieval distribution.

This study compares attention-to-MLP ratios in small decoder models on language modelling and synthetic retrieval tasks. The results are specific to the tested model sizes, training setup, and task design.
