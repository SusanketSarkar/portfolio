A small-model experiment in residual-stream depth

I wanted to know whether every layer contributes equally to a model’s predictions, or whether some depths carry information the rest of the network cannot easily replace. I tested that by adding controlled noise to one residual-stream slice at a time, then comparing the trained models with clean models perturbed only during evaluation.

114 training runs 8 and 16 layers 531M corpus tokens 3 seeds for anchor comparisons
In brief

The clearest result comes from perturbing a trained model at evaluation: the middle layers are more sensitive than the first, in both model sizes. That supports a depth-dependent information-density picture. Noise during training gives a different, more qualified result: at high dose it slows learning, and the most affected layer depends on model depth. It should not be read as a direct measure of information density.

01

Why look at depth?

A language model passes a running representation—the residual stream—through a stack of layers. A simple question follows: does each layer add information that matters equally to the final prediction, or are some depths harder to replace?

I used noise as a controlled way to ask that. If disturbing one slice changes the model’s predictions more than disturbing another, that slice is more consequential in that measurement. But there are two different measurements here. Noise added after training tests the model’s immediate dependence on a layer. Noise used during training lets the model adapt, so its result also reflects how noise changes learning.

To keep in mind: “A larger penalty means this intervention disrupted the model more; it does not, by itself, tell us why.”

02

Setup

I trained two decoder-only language models on the same WebEdu corpus. For each run, Gaussian noise was added to the residual stream immediately after attention in one selected layer. The noise was present during training only; evaluation was clean. A separate evaluation-time probe perturbed one layer of an already-trained plain model.

The intervention added zero-mean Gaussian noise after the attention sublayer in a chosen layer. Its standard deviation was σ times the local residual-stream RMS, so the dose was relative to the representation’s scale at that depth. Training runs used noise only during training; the separate sensitivity probe applied noise only at evaluation.

How much variation comes from the seed?

Three plain runs give a reference for run-to-run variation. The green band in the depth plots marks that scale: about ±0.0049 val_bpb for the 8-layer model and ±0.0017 for the 16-layer model. Small shifts inside the band are hard to distinguish from seed variation; large, repeated shifts are easier to interpret.

Where does the noise hurt?

Each point compares a model trained with noise at one layer against the plain model at the same noise setting. The vertical axis is the change in clean evaluation loss (Δval_bpb); higher means the noise-trained model performed worse. The green band marks the plain-run seed variation. 05

How does the effect change with noise strength?

These plots compare three representative positions—first, middle and last—as the noise scale rises. The horizontal axis is σ, the noise standard deviation relative to the local stream RMS; the vertical axis is the change in clean val_bpb versus the plain run. Near zero means little detectable cost; a sharp rise means the model has trouble learning with that dose.

Across both models, the strong-dose result is robust relative to the seed floor. The lower-dose changes are small and often comparable to that floor. This is evidence about disruption during training, not a clean ranking of stored information.

06

What are the layers actually carrying?

To understand the plain models themselves, I also measured two things: how much clean loss rises when a layer’s output is ablated, and how the logit-lens prediction changes as representations move through the stack. These are context for the noise experiments, not direct measures of information density.

Ablation effects are concentrated at the first layer, while the logit-lens loss falls steadily with depth. Neither curve alone reproduces the middle-peaked evaluation-time sensitivity. That is a reminder that “importance” depends on the probe.

the scale null

Output-projection weight norm is a confound: bigger attn.proj/mlp.down means the sublayer writes more into the stream, so (a) its noise matters more and (b) its ablation looks worse. Correlating fragility against that norm is the null we must clear before claiming a computational story about the middle. M (8L, ~29M params) and D (16L, ~52M params), same width (d=512), same corpus and optimizer. Depth is the only thing that changes between the two, so a pattern that holds at both scales is a depth effect, not a size effect. The key scale-dependent result: at strong noise, the 8L model is most fragile at the first layer while the 16L model is most fragile at the last layer.

07

Did noise hurt generalization, or learning?

A worse validation score alone cannot tell us whether a model learned less overall or learned a worse train-to-validation relationship. I therefore split the training-time result into train NLL and gen_gap = val_nll − train_nll.

Audit of the 18 σ=1.0 anchor runs: gen_gap stays between about −0.48 and −0.52, while the increase in validation loss is approximately matched by the increase in training loss in every run. The penalty is therefore best described as learning disruption or underfitting in this training budget, not a differential generalization failure.

The training curves show why that reading fits: train loss falls similarly at first, then noisy runs tend to finish higher. Validation loss also improves throughout, but noisy runs remain behind. The models are not converged, so this experiment measures early-learning behavior.

08

What the experiment supports

Information density varies by depth in these models. In the no-retraining evaluation probe, middle layers produce the largest loss increase when perturbed, and the first layer the smallest, in both the 8- and 16-layer models. This is the strongest evidence for the headline: the middle representation is less replaceable under this intervention.

Training-time fragility is a separate result. At σ=1.0, noise reliably disrupts learning, but the location depends on depth: first layer in the 8-layer model, last layer in the 16-layer model. The train-loss/gen-gap audit shows this is largely a learning-speed effect, not evidence that those layers contain more or less information. Lower-dose findings are weaker because many effects approach the seed variation.

How far should we generalize?

  • The 8-layer model saw 163.8M tokens and the 16-layer model 196.6M from the 531M-token WebEdu corpus—about 0.31 and 0.37 passes through the data. Both are undertrained, so the training-time result describes this learning regime.
  • These are small models (about 29M and 55M parameters) trained on one educational-text corpus. Seeing the evaluation-time pattern at both depths is encouraging, but larger models and broader data remain open questions.
  • Each run perturbed one layer. The all-layer controls show much larger penalties (about +0.41 and +1.48 val_bpb at σ=1.0), but they do not isolate which combinations cause the increase.
  • The seed reference differs by scale: ±0.0049 for 8 layers and ±0.0017 for 16 layers. Interpret “large” relative to the corresponding model’s floor.

The short answer is yes: the evaluation-time probe points to a middle peak in information density. The training-time experiment adds a different finding—noise can disrupt learning in a depth-dependent way—but it should not be used as a proxy for density.
