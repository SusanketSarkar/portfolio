I test how layer order affects language modeling: when does the familiar alternating pattern help, and when does a task reward a different schedule?

TL;DR

The best arrangement depends on the task. On the natural-text edu_mix benchmark, interleaved layouts form the strongest group; none of the five hand-designed depth-block layouts beats the four random interleavings. The standard alternating layout is a solid default, though it is not the single best run.

On a synthetic one-hop recall task, putting attention near both ends of the network performs much better than concentrating it in the middle. Separately, replacing up to half the attention sublayers with MLP sublayers changes natural-text validation loss only slightly in these experiments, while measured FLOPs fall by 1.2–3.6%.

Practical answer: keep attention and MLPs interleaved for broad language modeling unless you have evidence for a task-specific schedule. For efficiency, reducing attention count is a promising option at this scale—but the savings and quality trade-off need rechecking at longer contexts and larger models.

01

What question does this experiment answer?

A transformer layer combines two operations. Attention lets one token use information from other positions. An MLP transforms features at each position independently. The usual GPT layout alternates them:

… → attention → MLP → attention → MLP → …

I ask whether a model does better when these operations are arranged differently by depth—for example, with attention concentrated in the middle or at the ends. I also ask whether the answer changes with the task.

I tested two distinct ideas. First, we rearranged the same number of attention and MLP sublayers, so parameter count and operation counts stayed fixed. Second, we reduced the number of attention sublayers and added MLP sublayers, matching parameters and measuring FLOPs. The first tests where operations go; the second tests whether some attention can be removed.

I used a common layout mechanism for the comparisons, including for the alternating baseline, to keep initialization and implementation consistent. My main natural-text experiment uses 48 sublayers (24 attention and 24 MLP), an 80.1M-parameter model, and three random seeds per layout. Smaller 16- and 32-sublayer runs check whether the broad pattern changes with depth.

Which layouts were compared?

Every Family 1 layout contains 24 attention and 24 MLP sublayers; only their order changes. Blue in the diagram is attention and orange is MLP. The random layouts are shuffled controls, while A–D and “sandwich” place operation types in chosen depth bands.

In shorthand: A puts MLPs at the ends and attention in the middle; B puts attention at the ends and MLPs in the middle; C uses MLP-only ends with an attention-heavy center; D uses attention-only ends with an MLP-heavy center. The sandwich layout has attention near the ends and an alternating core.

04

Does the task change which layout wins?

In at least one clear case, yes. On a synthetic one-hop recall task, the model must use a repeated token association—roughly, if the sequence contains a → b and later asks about a, predict b. Here, attention at the ends (B and D) reaches nearly perfect exact-match recall, while A reaches 0.41.

The other synthetic tasks are less discriminating. All layouts saturate the local-rule task at exact match 1.00, and all are near-saturated on the two-hop task (0.992–0.998). When every layout solves a task, that task cannot tell us much about which arrangement is better.

What this answers
There is no universal winner in these experiments. Interleaving is strongest on the natural-text comparison, while attention at both ends is especially effective for this one-hop recall pattern. A schedule should be judged against its target task, not treated as an architectural law.

How to read the probes
The measurements show that layouts alter where context sensitivity, prediction quality, and update size appear. Together they make the task results easier to interpret. They do not justify assigning a fixed “job title” to every depth band: the probes are approximate and interact with computation from earlier layers.

How far does this efficiency result go?
These runs use sequence length 256 and width 512. At this context length, attention is only part of total computation, so the measured FLOP savings are modest. Longer contexts may change the trade-off, and this study does not measure memory use or establish performance at larger scales.
07

Does the natural-text pattern hold at other depths?

The broad split persists in the 16-, 32-, and 48-sublayer sweeps: designed layout C is last at all three depths, and the strongest results come from the interleaved group. The exact winner changes—baseline at 16, sandwich at 32, and rand1 at 48—so these experiments do not identify one universally optimal ordering.

08

So, what is the answer?

Attention and MLP placement matters, but the useful arrangement depends on the workload. In this study, interleaving is the safest choice for natural text: the alternating baseline is competitive, and several shuffled interleavings do better than the hand-designed blocks. On the one-hop recall task, attention near both ends is much better than attention concentrated in the middle.

Reducing attention count is another plausible lever. Up to half the attention sublayers were replaced with MLPs with only a small observed change in validation loss and a modest reduction in measured FLOPs. That is encouraging evidence for this model size and context—not a guarantee that attention can be removed freely in other settings.

09

What should readers keep in mind?

  • Scale: models range from 8M to 80M parameters and use sequence length 256. Larger models or longer contexts may behave differently.
  • Seeds: the main 48-sublayer comparison uses three seeds per layout. The measured noise floor helps contextualize effects, but estimates remain limited.
  • Coverage: the natural-text result uses one corpus, and the clear synthetic contrast is one-hop recall. Other data and tasks may favor other schedules.
  • Probes: context sensitivity, logit lens, and ablation are useful diagnostics with limitations. They do not isolate causal function perfectly.
  • Compute: reported FLOPs are measured, but memory and wall-clock speed are not the focus of this comparison.

What do the next-token distributions show?

For several prompts, the correct answer is not the most likely next token for any layout. For “The sun rises in the,” the top prediction is sky across the board, even though the scored canonical continuation is east. For “The capital of France is,” layouts mostly favor the, beginning a familiar phrase rather than directly predicting Paris. On the coreference prompt, the intended answer is dog, but many models favor cat. These examples show why I include teacher-forced NLL alongside top-1 predictions: NLL measures how much probability the model assigns to the full reference continuation, even when another token wins the argmax.

On the induction prompt (“The cat sat on the mat. The dog sat on the”), every layout puts mat first, with probabilities from 70% to 98%. That is a much easier prompt for these checkpoints than the factual and coreference examples. The longer sampled continuations are also revealing but noisy: with a fixed sampling seed they differ across layouts, and some repeat phrases or drift off-topic. I treat them as illustrations of generation style, not as reliable quality rankings.

How to interpret this appendix
These examples come from one seed and one checkpoint per layout, and the prompts were selected for illustration. They are useful for seeing concrete behavior, but they cannot establish general superiority. The corpus-level, multi-seed validation results in the main article remain the basis for the broad natural-text comparison.

I ran this small-scale controlled study of sublayer ordering and attention count. The results support a practical default—interleave for broad text modeling—and a research direction: test task-specific schedules and attention reductions against the actual workload.
